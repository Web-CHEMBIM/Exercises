MODULE OVERVIEW
================================================================================

At the end of this module, students should be able to:

Section 17.1 - Circuit Diagrams & Components:
  * Draw and interpret circuit diagrams containing cells, batteries, power
    supplies, generators, potential dividers, switches, resistors (fixed and
    variable), heaters, thermistors, LDRs, lamps, motors, bells, ammeters,
    voltmeters, magnetising coils, transformers, fuses and relays.
  * Describe how thermistors and LDRs behave in circuits.
  * [Supplement] Draw and interpret circuit diagrams containing diodes and LEDs.

Section 17.2 - Series Circuits:
  * Know that current at every point in a series circuit is the same.
  * Calculate the combined e.m.f. of several sources in series.
  * [Supplement] Know that the total p.d. across components in series equals
    the sum of individual p.d.s.
  * Calculate the combined resistance of two or more resistors in series.

Section 17.3 - Parallel Circuits:
  * Know how to construct and use parallel circuits.
  * State that current from the source is larger than current in each branch.
  * [Supplement] Recall and use the junction rule for currents.
  * [Supplement] Know that p.d. across parallel branches is the same.
  * State that combined resistance of two resistors in parallel is less than
    either resistor.
  * [Supplement] Calculate combined resistance of two resistors in parallel.
  * State the advantages of connecting lamps in parallel in a lighting circuit.

Section 17.4 - Potential Dividers:
  * Know that p.d. across a conductor increases as resistance increases for
    constant current.
  * [Supplement] Describe the action of a variable potential divider.
  * [Supplement] Recall and use the equation R₁/R₂ = V₁/V₂.

Section 17.5 - Electrical Safety:
  * State the hazards of damaged insulation, overheating cables, damp
    conditions, and excess current from overloading.
  * Know that a mains circuit consists of a live wire, neutral wire and earth
    wire; explain why a switch must be connected to the live wire.
  * Explain the use and operation of trip switches and fuses.
  * Explain why the outer casing of an electrical appliance must be either
    non-conducting (double-insulated) or earthed.
  * State that a fuse without an earth wire protects the circuit and cabling
    for a double-insulated appliance.

17.1.2  THERMISTORS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

> DEFINITION
> A thermistor is a resistor whose resistance changes with temperature.

For an NTC (Negative Temperature Coefficient) thermistor:
* As TEMPERATURE INCREASES → RESISTANCE DECREASES
* As TEMPERATURE DECREASES → RESISTANCE INCREASES

This property makes thermistors useful in temperature-sensing circuits, such as:
* Thermostats
* Fire alarms
* Temperature-controlled fans

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
17.2.1  WHAT IS A SERIES CIRCUIT?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

> DEFINITION
> A series circuit is a circuit with only one path for current to flow.

In a series circuit:
* All components are connected in a single loop
* The same current flows through every component
* If one component breaks, the whole circuit stops working

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
17.2.2  CURRENT IN SERIES CIRCUITS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

> KEY RULE
> In a series circuit, the current is the SAME at every point.

Why is this true?
* Current is the flow of electrons
* Electrons cannot be created or destroyed in the circuit
* The same number of electrons per second passes through each component

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                    CURRENT IN A SERIES CIRCUIT                              │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                             │
│    I₁ = I₂ = I₃ = ... = I (everywhere in the circuit)                       │
│                                                                             │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Factors affecting current in a series circuit:
1. VOLTAGE of power source: Higher voltage → more current
2. RESISTANCE in circuit: More components → more resistance → less current

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
17.2.3  E.M.F. IN SERIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

When cells or batteries are connected in series:

> KEY RULE
> The combined e.m.f. equals the SUM of individual e.m.f.s.

FORMULA:
                    E_total = E₁ + E₂ + E₃ + ...

EXAMPLE:
If three 1.5 V cells are connected in series:
    E_total = 1.5 + 1.5 + 1.5 = 4.5 V


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
17.2.4  POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE IN SERIES [SUPPLEMENT]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Extended Tier Only

> KEY RULE
> In a series circuit, the sum of p.d.s across all components equals the
> total e.m.f. of the power source.

FORMULA:
                    E = V₁ + V₂ + V₃ + ...

where:
    E = e.m.f. of power source (in V)
    V₁, V₂, V₃ = p.d. across each component (in V)

This means the e.m.f. is "shared" between the components in series.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WORKED EXAMPLE 1: Finding Missing P.D.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM:
A power source has an e.m.f. of 12 V. In a series circuit, the p.d. across
resistor X is 8 V. Calculate the p.d. across resistor Y.

SOLUTION:

Step 1: Identify given values and required quantity
    Given:   E = 12 V
             V_X = 8 V
    Required: V_Y = ?

Step 2: Recall the rule for p.d. in series
    E = V_X + V_Y

Step 3: Rearrange and substitute
    V_Y = E - V_X
    V_Y = 12 - 8
    V_Y = 4 V

Step 4: State the answer
    The p.d. across resistor Y is 4 V.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
17.2.5  RESISTANCE IN SERIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

> KEY RULE
> The combined resistance of resistors in series equals the SUM of the
> individual resistances.

FORMULA:
                    R_total = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...

where:
    R_total = total resistance (in Ω)
    R₁, R₂, R₃ = resistance of each resistor (in Ω)

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
17.3.1  WHAT IS A PARALLEL CIRCUIT?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

> DEFINITION
> A parallel circuit is a circuit with multiple paths (branches) for
> current to flow.

In a parallel circuit:
* Components are connected in separate branches
* Current splits at junctions and recombines
* If one branch breaks, current can still flow through other branches

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
17.3.2  CURRENT IN PARALLEL CIRCUITS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

> KEY RULE
> The current from the source is LARGER than the current in each branch.

At a junction (where wires meet):
* Current SPLITS into different branches
* Current RECOMBINES when branches join

JUNCTION RULE [SUPPLEMENT]:
Extended Tier Only

> The sum of currents entering a junction equals the sum of currents
> leaving the junction.

FORMULA:
                    I_in = I_out
                    I_total = I₁ + I₂ + I₃ + ...

This is because charge is conserved - electrons cannot be created or destroyed.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WORKED EXAMPLE 3: Current at a Junction
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM:
In a parallel circuit, ammeter A₀ shows a reading of 10 A, and ammeter A₁
shows 6 A. Calculate the reading on ammeter A₂.

SOLUTION:

Step 1: Recall the junction rule
    The total current entering a junction equals the total current leaving.

Step 2: Apply the junction rule
    I₀ = I₁ + I₂

Step 3: Rearrange and substitute
    I₂ = I₀ - I₁
    I₂ = 10 - 6
    I₂ = 4 A

Step 4: State the answer
    The reading on ammeter A₂ is 4 A.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
17.3.3  POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE IN PARALLEL [SUPPLEMENT]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Extended Tier Only

> KEY RULE
> The p.d. across each branch in a parallel circuit is the SAME.

FORMULA:
                    V₁ = V₂ = V₃ = ... = E

where:
    V₁, V₂, V₃ = p.d. across each branch (in V)
    E = e.m.f. of power source (in V)

This means each branch receives the full voltage of the power supply.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
17.3.4  RESISTANCE IN PARALLEL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

> KEY RULE
> The combined resistance of two resistors in parallel is LESS than the
> resistance of either resistor alone.

Why is this true?
* In parallel, there are more paths for current to flow
* More paths means less overall resistance to current

SPECIAL CASE: Two identical resistors in parallel
    Combined resistance = half of one resistor

    Example: Two 4 Ω resistors in parallel → R_total = 2 Ω

CALCULATING RESISTANCE IN PARALLEL [SUPPLEMENT]:
Extended Tier Only

FORMULA:
                         1          1       1
                    ─────────  =  ───── + ─────
                     R_total       R₁      R₂

Steps to calculate:
1. Calculate 1/R₁ and 1/R₂
2. Add them together to get 1/R_total
3. Take the reciprocal to find R_total

WORKED EXAMPLE 4: Resistance in Parallel [SUPPLEMENT]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM:
A 6 Ω resistor and a 3 Ω resistor are connected in parallel.
Calculate the combined resistance.

SOLUTION:

Step 1: Identify given values
    R₁ = 6 Ω
    R₂ = 3 Ω

Step 2: Apply the formula for resistors in parallel
       1          1       1
    ─────────  =  ───── + ─────
     R_total       R₁      R₂

Step 3: Substitute values
       1          1       1
    ─────────  =  ───── + ─────
     R_total       6       3

Step 4: Find common denominator and add
       1          1       2       3
    ─────────  =  ───── + ───── = ─────
     R_total       6       6       6

Step 5: Take the reciprocal
                    6
    R_total  =  ───── = 2 Ω
                    3

Step 6: State the answer
    The combined resistance is 2 Ω.

Note: The combined resistance (2 Ω) is less than either individual resistor.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
17.4.1  RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN P.D. AND RESISTANCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

When current flows through resistors in series:

> KEY RULE
> For a constant current, the p.d. across a component increases as its
> resistance increases.

This follows from Ohm's law: V = IR
* If I is constant and R increases, V must increase
* The component with the higher resistance has the larger p.d.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
17.4.2  VARIABLE POTENTIAL DIVIDERS [SUPPLEMENT]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Extended Tier Only

> DEFINITION
> A potential divider is a circuit that uses resistors in series to split
> the voltage from a power source.

A potential divider allows you to obtain a fraction of the total voltage.

HOW IT WORKS:
* Two resistors are connected in series
* The p.d. splits between them in the same ratio as their resistances
* A voltmeter or component connected in parallel measures/uses part of the voltage

If one resistor is variable (or is a thermistor/LDR):
* Changing its resistance changes how the voltage is divided
* This can be used to control other components automatically

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
17.4.3  POTENTIAL DIVIDER EQUATION [SUPPLEMENT]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Extended Tier Only

> KEY EQUATION
> R₁/R₂ = V₁/V₂

where:
    R₁ = resistance of resistor 1 (in Ω)
    R₂ = resistance of resistor 2 (in Ω)
    V₁ = p.d. across resistor 1 (in V)
    V₂ = p.d. across resistor 2 (in V)

The voltage splits in the SAME RATIO as the resistances.

Remember: V₁ + V₂ = E (total e.m.f.)


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WORKED EXAMPLE 5: Potential Divider Calculation [SUPPLEMENT]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM:
A potential divider has R₁ = 12 kΩ and R₂ = 20 kΩ.
The output voltage V_out (across R₂) is 5.0 V.
Calculate the input voltage V_in.

SOLUTION:

Step 1: Identify given values
    R₁ = 12 kΩ
    R₂ = 20 kΩ
    V₂ = V_out = 5.0 V

Step 2: Use the potential divider equation
    R₁/R₂ = V₁/V₂

Step 3: Substitute and solve for V₁
    12/20 = V₁/5.0
    V₁ = (12/20) × 5.0
    V₁ = 3.0 V

Step 4: Calculate total input voltage
    V_in = V₁ + V₂
    V_in = 3.0 + 5.0
    V_in = 8.0 V

Step 5: State the answer
    The input voltage is 8.0 V.

17.5.2  MAINS CIRCUITS - THE THREE WIRES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A mains circuit consists of THREE wires:

┌─────────────────┬─────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Wire            │ Colour      │ Function                                     │
├─────────────────┼─────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ LIVE            │ Brown       │ Carries current to the appliance             │
│                 │             │ At mains voltage (230 V)                     │
│                 │             │ MOST DANGEROUS wire                          │
├─────────────────┼─────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ NEUTRAL         │ Blue        │ Completes the circuit                        │
│                 │             │ At 0 V (or close to 0 V)                     │
├─────────────────┼─────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ EARTH           │ Green/Yellow│ Safety wire                                  │
│                 │ stripes     │ Provides low-resistance path to ground       │
│                 │             │ Only carries current during a fault          │
└─────────────────┴─────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────────────┘

> IMPORTANT
> The switch MUST be connected to the LIVE wire.

Why?
* When the switch is off, no voltage reaches the appliance
* This makes it safe to touch or repair the appliance
* If the switch was on the neutral wire, the appliance would still be "live"
  even when switched off

17.5.3  FUSES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

> DEFINITION
> A fuse is a safety device containing a thin wire that melts if the current
> becomes too large, breaking the circuit.

How fuses work:
1. Normal operation: Current flows through the fuse wire
2. Fault (excess current): Wire heats up and MELTS
3. Circuit broken: No current flows, appliance is disconnected

FUSE RATINGS:
* Common values: 3 A, 5 A, 13 A
* Choose the rating JUST ABOVE the normal operating current

EXAMPLE:
An appliance normally uses 2.5 A
* 3 A fuse: Correct choice (just above 2.5 A)
* 5 A fuse: Too high (would allow dangerous current before blowing)
* 13 A fuse: Far too high (could cause fire before blowing)

> KEY POINT
> A fuse protects the circuit and cabling, NOT the user.
> For a double-insulated appliance, the fuse protects the circuit and cables.

17.5.4  TRIP SWITCHES (CIRCUIT BREAKERS)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

> DEFINITION
> A trip switch (circuit breaker) is a safety device that automatically
> switches off if the current becomes too large.

Advantages of trip switches over fuses:
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Trip Switches                    │ Fuses                                   │
├──────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Can be RESET after tripping      │ Must be REPLACED after blowing          │
│ Reusable                         │ One-time use                             │
│ React faster                     │ Slower response                          │
└──────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────────┘


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
17.5.5  EARTHING AND DOUBLE INSULATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EARTHING:
For appliances with METAL cases:
* The earth wire connects the metal case to the ground
* If a live wire touches the case:
    1. Current flows through earth wire (low resistance path)
    2. Large surge of current causes fuse to blow
    3. Circuit is disconnected, preventing electric shock

DOUBLE INSULATION:
For appliances with PLASTIC cases (non-conducting outer):
* No earth wire is needed
* The plastic case cannot become live
* Two layers of insulation protect the user:
    1. Insulation around the wires
    2. Non-conducting outer case

> KEY POINT
> An appliance must be EITHER:
> - Earthed (metal case connected to earth wire), OR
> - Double-insulated (non-conducting outer case)
