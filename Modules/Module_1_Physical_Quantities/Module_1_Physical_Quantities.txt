1.1.1  MEASURING LENGTH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

INTRODUCTION
---------------
Measurement is fundamental to physics. Every experiment involves measuring
quantities - and the accuracy of your results depends on using the right
instrument and technique.

> DEFINITION
> A measurement is a quantitative observation - it gives a numerical value
> to a physical quantity.


CHOOSING THE RIGHT INSTRUMENT
-------------------------------

Different instruments are used depending on the size of the length being
measured:

┌──────────────────────────┬────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────┐
│ Instrument               │ Range                  │ Resolution              │
├──────────────────────────┼────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┤
│ Ruler                    │ Up to 100 cm           │ 1 mm (0.1 cm)           │
├──────────────────────────┼────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┤
│ Tape measure             │ Up to several metres   │ 1 mm                    │
├──────────────────────────┼────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┤
│ Trundle wheel            │ Tens of metres         │ The circumference of    │
│                          │                        │ the wheel (~1 m)        │
└──────────────────────────┴────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────┘

KEY POINTS:
  - A ruler can measure small distances of a few centimetres, to the nearest
    millimetre
  - A tape measure is used for longer lengths of tens of centimetres to a
    few metres
  - A trundle wheel is used for even larger distances (tens of metres), such
    as the length of a sports field


AVOIDING ERRORS WHEN MEASURING LENGTH
---------------------------------------

1. PARALLAX ERROR:
   - Always read the scale at EYE LEVEL, directly in line with the mark
   - Looking from an angle gives a false reading

2. ZERO ERROR:
   - Check that the ruler reads zero before the measurement starts
   - If the ruler is worn at the end, start measuring from a known
     graduation (e.g. 1 cm) and subtract it from your reading

3. For very small objects, measure several together and divide:
   - For example, measure the thickness of 10 sheets of paper, then
     divide by 10 to find the thickness of one sheet

WORKED EXAMPLE 1: Measuring Small Objects
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM:
The diagram shows four identical ball-bearings placed between two blocks
on a steel ruler. Calculate the diameter of one ball-bearing.

The blocks mark the edges of the first and last ball-bearings.
The left block is at the 4 cm mark. The right block is at the 12 cm mark.

SOLUTION:

Step 1: Measure the total length of all four ball-bearings
    Total length = 12 cm − 4 cm
    Total length = 8 cm

Step 2: Calculate the diameter of one ball-bearing
    Diameter = Total length ÷ Number of ball-bearings
    Diameter = 8 ÷ 4
    Diameter = 2 cm

EXAMINER TIP:
When measuring several small identical objects together, always divide the
total measurement by the number of objects to find the size of one.

1.1.2  MEASURING VOLUME
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MEASURING THE VOLUME OF A LIQUID
----------------------------------

A measuring cylinder is used to measure the volume of a liquid.

KEY POINTS:
  - Read the scale at the BOTTOM of the meniscus (the curved surface)
  - Read at EYE LEVEL to avoid parallax error
  - The units are usually cubic centimetres (cm³) or millilitres (ml)
    (1 cm³ = 1 ml)


MEASURING THE VOLUME OF AN IRREGULAR SOLID
---------------------------------------------

The volume of an irregular solid (e.g. a stone or key) can be found using
the DISPLACEMENT METHOD:

PROCEDURE:
  1. Pour water into a measuring cylinder and record the initial volume (V₁)
  2. Carefully lower the object into the water
  3. Record the new volume (V₂)
  4. Calculate the volume of the object:

         Volume of object = V₂ − V₁

1.1.3  MEASURING TIME
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

INSTRUMENTS FOR MEASURING TIME
---------------------------------

In physics, stop-clocks and stopwatches are used to measure time intervals.

KEY POINTS:
  - Before starting, ensure the stopwatch is reset to zero
  - Digital stopwatches typically measure to the nearest 0.01 s
  - Analogue stop-clocks typically measure to the nearest 0.5 s


HUMAN REACTION TIME
---------------------

An important factor when measuring time intervals is human reaction time:

  - The typical human reaction time for an alert person is about 0.25 s
  - This creates an uncertainty at the START and at the END of timing
  - Total reaction time error could be up to 0.5 s per measurement

For this reason, very short time intervals should be measured carefully
using techniques to reduce the impact of reaction time.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WORKED EXAMPLE 2: Measuring Time with a Stopwatch
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM:
A stopwatch is used to measure the time taken for a runner to complete a
lap of a 400 m track.

The stopwatch shows 0:55:10 at the start and 1:45:10 at the end.
Calculate how long it took the runner to complete the lap.

SOLUTION:

Step 1: Identify the start time
    Start time = 55.10 s

Step 2: Identify the finish time
    Finish time = 1 minute and 45.10 s

Step 3: Convert the finish time into seconds
    1 minute = 60 seconds
    Finish time = 60 s + 45.10 s = 105.10 s

Step 4: Calculate the total time taken
    Total time = Finish time − Start time
    Total time = 105.10 s − 55.10 s
    Total time = 50 s

EXAMINER TIP:
Sometimes information is given in the question that is NOT needed for
the calculation (e.g. the 400 m track length here). This is a common
technique to make a question seem harder. Don't let it catch you out!


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.1.4  MULTIPLE READINGS AND FIDUCIAL MARKERS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHY TAKE MULTIPLE READINGS?
-----------------------------

Taking multiple readings in physics reduces the impact of measurement errors:

1. FOR VERY SMALL MEASUREMENTS:
   - The thickness of a single sheet of paper is too small to measure
     accurately with a ruler
   - Measure the thickness of 100 sheets, then divide by 100
   - This gives a much more accurate average value for one sheet

2. FOR TIME MEASUREMENTS:
   - A single timing of a pendulum swing includes reaction time errors
   - Instead, time 10 or 20 complete swings
   - Divide the total time by the number of swings
   - This reduces the percentage error from reaction time


FIDUCIAL MARKERS
------------------

> DEFINITION
> A fiducial marker is a visual reference point, such as a line or a dot,
> that marks the position at which a measurement is taken.

When timing oscillations (e.g. of a pendulum):
  - Place a fiducial marker at the equilibrium (rest) position
  - This is the point where the pendulum moves fastest
  - It makes it easier to see when the pendulum passes this point
  - This reduces the error in starting and stopping the timer
  - Always start and stop timing as the pendulum passes the marker
    in the SAME direction

1.2.1  SCALAR AND VECTOR QUANTITIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

INTRODUCTION
---------------
In physics, every quantity can be classified as one of two types: a scalar
or a vector. Understanding the difference is essential for solving problems
involving motion and forces.

> DEFINITION
> A scalar quantity has magnitude (size) only.

> DEFINITION
> A vector quantity has both magnitude (size) and direction.

EXAMPLES:
  - Mass is a SCALAR: it has a value (e.g. 5 kg) but no direction
  - Weight is a VECTOR: it has a value (e.g. 50 N) AND a direction (downward)


TABLE OF COMMON SCALARS AND VECTORS:

1.2.2  DISTANCE AND DISPLACEMENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

DISTANCE:
  - A measure of how far an object has travelled, regardless of direction
  - It is the total length of the path taken
  - Distance has magnitude but NO direction
  - Distance is a SCALAR quantity

DISPLACEMENT:
  - A measure of the straight-line distance between two points, INCLUDING
    direction
  - Displacement is the length and direction of a straight line drawn from
    the starting point to the finishing point
  - Displacement has magnitude AND direction
  - Displacement is a VECTOR quantity


EXAMPLE:
A student walks 300 m north and then 400 m east to get to school.

  - Distance travelled = 300 + 400 = 700 m (the total path)
  - Displacement = the straight-line distance from home to school
    (which can be calculated using Pythagoras' theorem: √(300² + 400²) = 500 m
    in a direction northeast)

1.2.3  SPEED AND VELOCITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SPEED:
  - A measure of the distance travelled per unit time
  - Speed describes how FAST an object is moving
  - Speed has magnitude but NO direction
  - Speed is a SCALAR quantity

VELOCITY:
  - A measure of the displacement per unit time, including direction
  - Velocity describes how fast an object is moving AND the direction
  - Velocity has magnitude AND direction
  - Velocity is a VECTOR quantity

KEY POINT:
An object can have a constant SPEED but a changing VELOCITY if it is
changing direction.

EXAMPLE:
A car driving around a roundabout at a constant speed of 30 km/h:
  - Its speed is constant (30 km/h)
  - But its velocity is constantly changing because its direction changes


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WORKED EXAMPLE 3: Mass vs Weight (Scalar vs Vector)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM:
An instructor is explaining the difference between mass and weight to
junior astronauts. Suggest how the instructor should explain the difference,
using definitions of scalars and vectors in your answer.

SOLUTION:

Step 1: Recall the definitions
    Scalars are quantities that have only magnitude.
    Vectors are quantities that have both magnitude and direction.

Step 2: Classify mass
    Mass is a quantity with magnitude only (e.g. 70 kg).
    Therefore, mass is a SCALAR quantity.
    The instructor might explain that their mass will NOT change as their
    location in the Universe changes.

Step 3: Classify weight
    Weight is a force, so it has both magnitude and direction.
    Therefore, weight is a VECTOR quantity.
    The instructor might explain that their weight (the gravitational force
    acting on them) WILL change depending on location. For example, the
    force of weight on the Moon is less than on Earth.

EXAMINER TIP:
Make sure you know the commonly confused pairings:
  - Distance and displacement
  - Speed and velocity
  - Mass and weight

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.3.1  VECTOR DIAGRAMS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

INTRODUCTION
---------------
Vectors can be represented visually using vector diagrams. These diagrams
use arrows to show both the magnitude and direction of a quantity.

RULES FOR DRAWING VECTORS:
  - The LENGTH of the arrow represents the magnitude
  - The DIRECTION of the arrow indicates the direction of the quantity
  - The scale of arrows should be proportional to the relative magnitudes
    (e.g. an arrow for 4 N should be twice as long as an arrow for 2 N)


COMPONENT VECTORS:
  - A single vector can be split into two components:
    • A HORIZONTAL component (FH)
    • A VERTICAL component (FV)
  - Component vectors are often drawn with dotted lines


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.3.2  COMBINING VECTORS GRAPHICALLY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

When two vectors act at right angles to each other, they can be combined
into a single RESULTANT vector.

THE GRAPHICAL METHOD:

Follow these steps:
  1. Choose a suitable SCALE (e.g. 1 cm = 10 N)
  2. Draw the first vector to scale in the correct direction
  3. Draw the second vector to scale at right angles
  4. Complete the rectangle
  5. Draw the RESULTANT vector as the diagonal from the origin
  6. Measure the length of the resultant and convert using the scale
  7. Use a protractor to measure the angle


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.3.3  COMBINING VECTORS BY CALCULATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Instead of drawing to scale, vectors can be combined using mathematics.

WHEN TWO VECTORS ARE PERPENDICULAR (at 90°):

1. Find the MAGNITUDE of the resultant using Pythagoras' theorem:

              R = √(F₁² + F₂²)

2. Find the DIRECTION (angle) using trigonometry:

                        opposite
              tan θ = ──────────
                        adjacent

Where θ is the angle between the resultant and one of the component vectors.

TRIGONOMETRY REMINDER (SOH CAH TOA):
  - sin θ = opposite / hypotenuse
  - cos θ = adjacent / hypotenuse
  - tan θ = opposite / adjacent

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WORKED EXAMPLE 4: Resultant Force Calculation
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM:
A force acts on an object with 60 N to the right. A second force of 100 N
acts on the same object in the upward direction. Calculate the resultant
force acting on the object.

SOLUTION:

Step 1: Draw a vector diagram (sketch)
    The two forces act at right angles to each other.
    F₁ = 60 N (horizontal, to the right)
    F₂ = 100 N (vertical, upward)

Step 2: Calculate the magnitude using Pythagoras' theorem
    R = √(F₁² + F₂²)
    R = √(60² + 100²)
    R = √(3600 + 10000)
    R = √13600
    R = 117 N (to 3 significant figures)

Step 3: Calculate the direction using trigonometry
                   opposite       100
    tan θ = ──────────── = ──────
                   adjacent        60

    θ = tan⁻¹(100/60)
    θ = 59° (to 2 significant figures)

Step 4: State the final answer with magnitude and direction
    The resultant force is 117 N at 59° from the horizontal.

EXAMINER TIP:
If the question asks you to use a specific method (graphical or calculation),
you MUST use that method. If the choice is yours, calculation is usually
quicker and more accurate.

SECTION 1.2: Scalars & Vectors

A6. A scalar quantity has magnitude (size) only.
    A vector quantity has both magnitude and direction.

A7. a) Vector (force with direction)
    b) Scalar (speed has no direction stated)
    c) Scalar (mass)
    d) Vector (displacement - has direction "north")
    e) Scalar (energy)

A8. Distance is the total length of the path taken (scalar, no direction).
    Displacement is the straight-line distance from start to finish,
    including direction (vector).

A9. Although the satellite has constant speed, its direction is
    continuously changing as it orbits. Velocity includes direction,
    so if direction changes, velocity changes. Therefore, velocity
    is not constant.

A10. Vectors: force, velocity (or weight, displacement, acceleration).
     Scalars: mass, energy (or distance, speed, temperature, volume).


SECTION 1.3: Calculating with Vectors

A11. R = √(30² + 40²) = √(900 + 1600) = √2500 = 50 N

A12. tan θ = 40 / 30
     θ = tan⁻¹(40/30) = tan⁻¹(1.333) = 53.1°

A13. a) Total distance = 5 + 12 = 17 km
     b) Displacement = √(5² + 12²) = √(25 + 144) = √169 = 13 km

A14. The graphical method relies on measuring the length of the resultant
     arrow. If the diagram is not drawn to scale, the measured length
     will give an incorrect magnitude.

A15. Pythagoras' theorem (for magnitude) and trigonometry (for direction).
