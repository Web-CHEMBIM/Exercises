Syllabus Reference: 1.1 Physical quantities and measurement techniques

Learning Objectives Covered:
  Core:
    1. Describe the use of rulers and measuring cylinders to find a
       length or a volume.
    2. Describe how to measure a variety of time intervals using clocks
       and digital timers.
    3. Determine an average value for a small distance and for a short
       interval of time by measuring multiples (including the period of
       oscillation of a pendulum).
  Supplement:
    4. Understand that a scalar quantity has magnitude (size) only and
       that a vector quantity has magnitude and direction.
    5. Know that the following quantities are scalars: distance, speed,
       time, mass, energy and temperature.
    6. Know that the following quantities are vectors: force, weight,
       velocity, acceleration, momentum, electric field strength and
       gravitational field strength.
    7. Determine, by calculation or graphically, the resultant of two
       vectors at right angles, limited to forces or velocities only.

Distribution:
  - Recall (AO1): 13 questions (26%)
  - Application (AO2): 25 questions (50%)
  - Analysis (AO3): 12 questions (24%)

SECTION H: COMPARE & CONTRAST

46. Both distance and displacement describe how far an object has moved
    [B1]. Distance is the total path length — it is a scalar and has
    no direction [B1]. Displacement is the straight-line distance from
    start to finish with direction — it is a vector [B1].
    Example: walking 300 m north then 400 m east gives distance = 700 m
    but displacement = 500 m in a northeast direction [B1].

47. Ruler: advantage — high resolution (1 mm), suitable for precise
    short measurements [B1]. Disadvantage — limited range (up to 1 m),
    impractical for large distances [B1].
    Trundle wheel: advantage — suitable for measuring long distances
    (tens of metres) quickly [B1]. Disadvantage — lower resolution
    (~1 m per click), unsuitable for precise small measurements [B1].

48. A scalar quantity has magnitude only; examples: mass and
    energy [B1]. A vector quantity has both magnitude and direction;
    examples: force and velocity [B1]. The key difference is that
    vectors require a direction to be fully described [B1]. Two
    scalar values are added arithmetically, whereas vectors must be
    added using vector diagrams or trigonometry [B1].
