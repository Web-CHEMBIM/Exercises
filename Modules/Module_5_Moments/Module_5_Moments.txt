================================================================================
                   SECTION 5.1: THE MOMENT OF A FORCE
================================================================================

HOOK - THINK ABOUT THIS:
Why is the door handle always placed far from the hinges? And why do you
sometimes need both hands to loosen a tight bolt with a short spanner,
but a long spanner makes it easy? The answer lies in moments.

RECALL:
• A force can make an object speed up, slow down, or change direction.
• What else can a force do? Forces can also make objects ROTATE.

5.1.1 WHAT IS A MOMENT?
------------------------

When a force is applied to an object at a distance from a fixed point (pivot),
it tends to rotate the object. This turning effect is called a MOMENT.

The MOMENT OF A FORCE is defined as:

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│           The turning effect of a force about a pivot.                      │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

The moment depends on TWO things:
1. The SIZE of the force
2. The PERPENDICULAR DISTANCE from the pivot to the line of action of the force

EVERYDAY EXAMPLES OF MOMENTS:

• A see-saw in a playground — a child pushes down, rotating the board
• Opening a door — your push on the handle rotates the door about its hinges
• Using a spanner to tighten or loosen a bolt
• Using a screwdriver to open a tin of paint
• Turning a tap on or off
• Picking up a wheelbarrow
• Scissors cutting paper — two blades rotating about a central pivot

5.1.2 THE MOMENT EQUATION
--------------------------

The SIZE of a moment is calculated using:

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                                                             │
│              moment = force × perpendicular distance from pivot             │
│                                                                             │
│                              M = F × d                    ... (5.1)        │
│                                                                             │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Where:
  M = moment (N m) — newton metres
  F = force (N) — newtons
  d = perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force (m)

IMPORTANT:
• The distance d must be the PERPENDICULAR distance — measured at 90° from
  the pivot to the line of action of the force.
• The unit of moment is the newton metre (N m).
• If distance is in centimetres, the unit becomes N cm — always convert to
  metres unless instructed otherwise.

DIRECTION OF ROTATION:
• A force applied to the RIGHT of the pivot tends to cause CLOCKWISE rotation.
• A force applied to the LEFT of the pivot tends to cause ANTICLOCKWISE rotation.
• Think of a clock face to decide the direction.

EFFECT OF INCREASING DISTANCE:
The further a force is applied from the pivot, the LARGER the moment.

This explains why:
• Door handles are placed far from the hinges — less force needed to open door
• A long spanner produces a larger turning effect than a short one
• Pushing a door near the hinge requires a very large force

WORKED EXAMPLE 5.1
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Problem:
A carpenter uses a spanner of length 20 cm to loosen a bolt. They apply a
force of 22 N at a point 5 cm from the end of the spanner.
Calculate the moment of the force about the bolt.

Solution:

Step 1: Identify the given quantities.
  Force, F = 22 N
  Length of spanner = 20 cm
  Force applied 5 cm from the end → distance from pivot = 20 − 5 = 15 cm

Step 2: Convert the distance to metres.
  d = 15 cm ÷ 100 = 0.15 m

Step 3: Write the moment equation.
  M = F × d

Step 4: Substitute values and calculate.
  M = 22 × 0.15
  M = 3.3 N m

Answer: The moment of the force is 3.3 N m (clockwise).

Key Points:
• Always convert cm to m before calculating if you want the answer in N m.
• The perpendicular distance is measured FROM THE PIVOT — not from the end
  of the spanner.

A student pushes down on a door handle with a force of 15 N. The handle is

================================================================================
                   SECTION 5.2: THE PRINCIPLE OF MOMENTS
================================================================================

HOOK - THINK ABOUT THIS:
Two children of different weights want to balance a see-saw. The heavier child
sits closer to the centre. How far away must the lighter child sit? This
section gives you the tools to answer exactly that question.

RECALL:
• A moment is the turning effect of a force: M = F × d
• Forces can cause clockwise or anticlockwise rotation
• For a beam to be balanced, something must stop it rotating in either direction

5.2.1 THE PRINCIPLE OF MOMENTS (CORE)
--------------------------------------

When a beam (or any object) is BALANCED, the rotation in each direction must
cancel out. This is stated as the Principle of Moments:

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  If an object is in equilibrium, the total clockwise moment about any       │
│  pivot equals the total anticlockwise moment about that pivot.              │
│                                                                             │
│         Total clockwise moment = Total anticlockwise moment                 │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

APPLYING THE PRINCIPLE (simple case — one force each side):

For a balanced beam with one force on each side of the pivot:

         F1 × d1 = F2 × d2        ... (5.2)

Where:
  F1 = anticlockwise force (N)
  d1 = perpendicular distance of F1 from pivot (m)
  F2 = clockwise force (N)
  d2 = perpendicular distance of F2 from pivot (m)

════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
WORKED EXAMPLE 5.2
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Problem:
A parent (weight 690 N) and child (weight 140 N) sit on opposite ends of a
see-saw. The parent sits 0.3 m from the pivot.
Calculate the distance the child must sit from the pivot to balance the see-saw.

Solution:

Step 1: Identify known quantities.
  Anticlockwise force (parent): Fadult = 690 N, dadult = 0.3 m
  Clockwise force (child): Fchild = 140 N, dchild = ?

Step 2: Write the principle of moments.
  Total clockwise moment = Total anticlockwise moment

Step 3: Calculate the anticlockwise moment (parent).
  Madult = 690 × 0.3 = 207 N m

Step 4: Apply the principle of moments.
  Fchild × dchild = Madult
  140 × dchild = 207

Step 5: Solve for dchild.
  dchild = 207 ÷ 140
  dchild = 1.48 m ≈ 1.5 m

Answer: The child must sit approximately 1.5 m from the pivot.

Key Points:
• The lighter person must sit FURTHER from the pivot.
• Always identify which force is clockwise and which is anticlockwise first.
• Ensure all distances are in the same unit (convert cm → m if needed).

5.2.2 THE PRINCIPLE OF MOMENTS WITH MULTIPLE FORCES [SUPPLEMENT]
-----------------------------------------------------------------

If more than one force acts on either side of the pivot, we calculate the
SUM of all clockwise moments and the SUM of all anticlockwise moments:

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│   Sum of clockwise moments = Sum of anticlockwise moments                   │
│                                                                             │
│   (F2 × d2) = (F1 × d1) + (F3 × d3)                       ... (5.3)       │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

WORKED EXAMPLE 5.3  [Supplement]
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Problem:
A beam is balanced on a pivot. Force F1 = 20 N acts 0.5 m to the left of
the pivot. Force F3 = 10 N acts 1.2 m to the left of the pivot.
Both F1 and F3 act downward (anticlockwise). Force F2 acts 0.8 m to the
right of the pivot. The beam is balanced. Find F2.

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate all anticlockwise moments.
  M1 = 20 × 0.5 = 10 N m
  M3 = 10 × 1.2 = 12 N m
  Total anticlockwise = 10 + 12 = 22 N m

Step 2: Apply principle of moments.
  F2 × 0.8 = 22

Step 3: Solve for F2.
  F2 = 22 ÷ 0.8 = 27.5 N

Answer: F2 = 27.5 N

5.2.3 EXPERIMENT: DEMONSTRATING THE PRINCIPLE OF MOMENTS [SUPPLEMENT]
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Aim: To verify that the net moment on a balanced beam is zero.

Equipment:
  • Metre ruler with a small hole at the centre
  • Clamp stand, boss and clamp
  • Optical pin and cork (pivot)
  • 2 × 100 g mass hangers + additional 100 g masses
  • Cotton thread loops (equal lengths)
  • Small piece of plasticine (to balance ruler initially)

Method:
  1. Balance the ruler horizontally on the pivot. Adjust with plasticine.
  2. Hang two unequal loads at different distances on either side.
  3. Adjust the distances until the beam is perfectly horizontal.
  4. Record mass, force (F = mg), and distance from pivot for each load.
  5. Repeat with different load combinations (at least 5 readings).

                        SECTION 5.3: EQUILIBRIUM
================================================================================

HOOK - THINK ABOUT THIS:
A book resting on a table is in equilibrium. A spinning top balanced on its
point is also in equilibrium (while spinning). What exactly does it mean for
something to be in equilibrium, and what are the conditions that must be met?

RECALL:
• A resultant force causes acceleration (Module 4)
• The principle of moments relates to balanced turning effects

5.3.1 CONDITIONS FOR EQUILIBRIUM
----------------------------------

An object is in EQUILIBRIUM when it is in a state of balance — it is not
accelerating (linearly or rotationally).

For an object to be in equilibrium, TWO conditions must BOTH be satisfied:

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  Condition 1: The resultant force on the object is ZERO.                   │
│               (The forces are balanced in all directions.)                  │
│                                                                             │
│  Condition 2: The resultant moment about any point is ZERO.                │
│               (Total clockwise moment = Total anticlockwise moment)         │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

EXAMPLES OF OBJECTS IN EQUILIBRIUM:

• A book resting on a table
  - Weight acts downward; normal reaction acts upward → no resultant force
  - No tendency to rotate → no resultant moment

• A balanced see-saw
  - Downward forces balanced by upward reaction at pivot
  - Clockwise and anticlockwise moments are equal

• A bridge
  - Weight of bridge and traffic balanced by upward forces at supports
  - Moments are balanced to prevent tilting

5.3.2 RECOGNISING EQUILIBRIUM AND NON-EQUILIBRIUM
----------------------------------------------------

OBJECT IN EQUILIBRIUM:
• Stationary object with balanced forces (book on table, person standing still)
• Object moving at constant velocity (zero net force, no rotation)
• Balanced beam or ruler

OBJECT NOT IN EQUILIBRIUM:
• Object accelerating due to unbalanced forces
• A beam that is tilting (net moment is not zero)
• An object that is rotating and speeding up or slowing down

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING 5.3
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

1. State the TWO conditions required for an object to be in equilibrium.

2. A book rests on a table. Is it in equilibrium? Justify your answer.

3. A see-saw has a 500 N person at 1.0 m to the left and a 200 N person
   at 1.0 m to the right. Is this see-saw in equilibrium? Explain.

ANSWERS:
1. (1) The resultant force on the object is zero (balanced forces).
   (2) The resultant moment about any point is zero (balanced moments).

2. Yes. The weight of the book (downward) is balanced by the normal reaction
   from the table (upward), so the resultant force is zero. There is no
   tendency to rotate, so the resultant moment is also zero.

3. No. Anticlockwise moment = 500 × 1.0 = 500 N m.
   Clockwise moment = 200 × 1.0 = 200 N m.
   500 N m ≠ 200 N m → unbalanced moment → see-saw tilts → NOT in equilibrium.

                      SECTION 5.4: CENTRE OF GRAVITY
================================================================================

HOOK - THINK ABOUT THIS:
Why does a racing car have such a low body? Why does a double-decker bus
risk tipping on a steep hill, while a sports car does not? The answer lies
in where the weight of the object effectively "acts from" — its centre of gravity.

RECALL:
• Weight is the gravitational force acting on an object (W = mg)
• For equilibrium, the resultant force and resultant moment must both be zero

5.4.1 DEFINITION OF CENTRE OF GRAVITY
---------------------------------------

Every object has weight distributed throughout it. However, for calculations,
we can treat all the weight as if it acts at a single point. This point is
called the CENTRE OF GRAVITY:

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│   The centre of gravity of an object is the point through which the        │
│   weight of the object acts.                                                │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

KEY POINTS:
• The centre of gravity is a hypothetical point — it doesn't have to be
  inside the object (think of a ring or a boomerang).
• For SYMMETRICAL objects of uniform density, the centre of gravity is at
  the point (or axis) of symmetry.
• Always draw weight forces as if they act from the centre of gravity.

5.4.2 EXPERIMENT: FINDING THE CENTRE OF GRAVITY OF AN IRREGULAR LAMINA
------------------------------------------------------------------------

An IRREGULAR PLANE LAMINA is a flat, thin shape that is not symmetrical.
We cannot just use symmetry to find its centre of gravity — we need an experiment.

Equipment:
  • Irregularly shaped plane lamina (e.g., a cut piece of cardboard)
  • Hole punch (to make small holes near the edge)
  • Thread and hanging mass or sticky tack (plumb line)
  • Pencil and ruler
  • Clamp stand, boss and clamp

Method:

  "CG = intersection of lines" at bottom

Step-by-Step Method:

1. Punch 3 holes near the outer edges of the lamina (at different locations).
2. Hang the lamina freely from a pin through hole 1.
   It will swing until its centre of gravity is directly below the pin.
3. Hang a plumb line from the same pin. Mark the line of the plumb line
   on the lamina using a pencil and ruler.
4. Repeat steps 2–3 by hanging from hole 2, then hole 3.
5. The three lines all cross at the SAME point — this is the centre of gravity.

Why This Works:
When an object is hung freely from a point, gravity pulls it so that the centre
of gravity is directly below the point of suspension. The plumb line marks the
vertical line through that suspension point. Where all such lines cross is the
one point that was always directly below every suspension point — the CG.

5.4.3 STABILITY AND CENTRE OF GRAVITY [SUPPLEMENT]
----------------------------------------------------

The POSITION of the centre of gravity determines how STABLE an object is.

DEFINITION OF STABILITY:
The ability of an object to return to its original position after being
slightly displaced (tilted or pushed).

RULE 1: AN OBJECT IS STABLE WHILE ITS CENTRE OF GRAVITY LIES ABOVE ITS BASE.

If the object is tilted slightly:
• If the line of action of the weight STILL passes through the base → restoring
  moment acts → object returns to upright → STABLE
• If the line of action of the weight falls OUTSIDE the base → toppling moment
  acts → object falls over → UNSTABLE

RULE 2: THE MOST STABLE OBJECTS HAVE:
  (a) A LOW centre of gravity
  (b) A WIDE base

Examples:

└────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────────────┘

ANGLE OF TILT:
A car, with its wider base and lower CG, can tilt to about 60° before toppling.
A tall bus, with its narrower base and higher CG, will topple at about 45°.

WAYS TO INCREASE STABILITY:
1. Lower the centre of gravity (e.g., fill the base with heavier material,
   lower the load)
2. Widen the base (e.g., spread the legs of a ladder or tripod further apart)

KEY CONCEPTS:

MOMENT OF A FORCE (5.1):
• Moment = turning effect of a force about a pivot
• M = F × d      (d must be the PERPENDICULAR distance)
• Unit: newton metre (N m)
• Direction: clockwise or anticlockwise
• Larger force further from pivot → larger moment

PRINCIPLE OF MOMENTS (5.2):
• For equilibrium: total clockwise moment = total anticlockwise moment
• Core: one force each side → F1 × d1 = F2 × d2
• Supplement: sum of CW moments = sum of ACW moments (multiple forces)
• Demonstrated by balancing a ruler with hanging masses

EQUILIBRIUM (5.3):
• TWO conditions:
  (1) Resultant force = 0 (balanced forces)
  (2) Resultant moment = 0 (balanced moments)
• Both conditions must be satisfied simultaneously

CENTRE OF GRAVITY (5.4):
• The point through which the weight of an object acts
• Regular, symmetrical objects: CG at point of symmetry
• Irregular lamina: found using suspension method (plumb line)
• [Supplement] Stability: object stable while CG line of action is inside base
• Most stable: low CG + wide base

KEY EQUATIONS:

Moment:                    M = F × d             ... (5.1)

Principle of moments:      F1 × d1 = F2 × d2     ... (5.2)

Principle of moments       Sum of CW moments =
(multiple forces):         Sum of ACW moments     ... (5.3)

================================================================================
                       PRACTICE EXERCISE ANSWERS
================================================================================

7. d = 25 cm = 0.25 m
   M = 40 × 0.25 = 10 N m

8. 12 × 0.5 = 8 × d
   6 = 8d
   d = 0.75 m

9. 300 × 2.0 = 600 × d
   600 = 600d
   d = 1.0 m

10. Yes. The support forces from the ground (upward) equal the weight (downward)
    and the weight of the cyclist → resultant force = 0. The handlebars are
    held steady → resultant moment = 0. Both conditions are satisfied.

11. Total ACW = (15 × 0.2) + (10 × 0.4) = 3 + 4 = 7 N m
    F × 0.5 = 7
    F = 14 N

12. Not always true. Widening the base improves stability ONLY if the CG is
    simultaneously kept low or does not rise significantly. If widening the
    base also raises the CG (e.g., a wider but taller structure), the net
    effect on stability depends on which factor dominates. Generally, the
    combination of LOW CG AND wide base gives the greatest stability.

13. The bus has a higher centre of gravity (tall body, heavy upper deck) and
    a narrower base (wheelbase) relative to its height. As the road steepens,
    the line of action of the bus's weight reaches beyond its narrow base at a
    smaller angle of tilt (~45°), causing it to topple. The car's lower CG
    and wider base (relative to height) means the line of action stays within
    the base to much steeper angles (~60°), so it remains stable longer.
