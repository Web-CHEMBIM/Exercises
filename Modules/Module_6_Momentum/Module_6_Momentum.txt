This module introduces momentum — a fundamental property of moving objects.
You will learn how to calculate momentum, why it is conserved in collisions,
how impulse relates to force and time, and how Newton's second law can be
expressed in terms of momentum.

• Express Newton's second law as F = Δp / Δt [Supplement]
• Describe force as the rate of change of momentum [Supplement]
• Apply F = Δp / Δt to solve problems [Supplement]

================================================================================
                      SECTION 6.1: MOMENTUM
================================================================================

HOOK — THINK ABOUT THIS:
A tennis ball flying at 200 km/h and a slow-moving lorry — both are hard to
stop suddenly, but for very different reasons. The ball because of its high
speed, the lorry because of its enormous mass. What single quantity captures
BOTH of these effects? The answer is momentum.

RECALL:
• Velocity is a vector — it has both magnitude (speed) and direction (Module 2).
• A scalar has magnitude only; a vector has magnitude AND direction.
• Force causes acceleration (change in velocity) — F = ma (Module 4).

6.1.1 DEFINING MOMENTUM
------------------------

Any object that has MASS and is MOVING has MOMENTUM.

Momentum is defined by the equation:

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                                                             │
│                         momentum = mass × velocity                         │
│                                                                             │
│                              p = m × v                     ... (6.1)       │
│                                                                             │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Where:
  p = momentum (kg m/s) — kilogram metres per second
  m = mass (kg) — kilograms
  v = velocity (m/s) — metres per second

KEY POINTS:
• An object AT REST has v = 0, therefore p = 0. No motion → no momentum.
• Momentum is a VECTOR quantity — it has both magnitude AND direction.
• Because velocity is a vector, momentum can be POSITIVE or NEGATIVE:
  - Positive momentum: moving in the chosen positive direction
  - Negative momentum: moving in the opposite direction

SIGN CONVENTION:
You choose which direction is positive. Once chosen, be consistent throughout.
• In most problems: RIGHT or UP = positive → LEFT or DOWN = negative.

6.1.2 EFFECT OF MASS AND VELOCITY ON MOMENTUM
----------------------------------------------

Momentum depends equally on BOTH mass and velocity:

• A heavy, slow object can have the same momentum as a light, fast object.
• A large mass at low speed may be just as hard to stop as a small mass
  at high speed.

Example — Comparing a tennis ball and a brick:
  Tennis ball: m = 0.06 kg, v = 75 m/s → p = 0.06 × 75 = 4.5 kg m/s
  Brick:       m = 3 kg,    v = 1.5 m/s → p = 3 × 1.5   = 4.5 kg m/s

Both have the SAME momentum despite very different masses and speeds. This
means they would exert a similar stopping force (given the same stopping time).

WORKED EXAMPLE 6.1
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Problem:
A car of mass 1200 kg travels east at 15 m/s. Calculate its momentum.
State the direction of the momentum.

Solution:

Step 1: Identify the known quantities.
  Mass, m = 1200 kg
  Velocity, v = +15 m/s (taking east as positive)
  Required: momentum, p

Step 2: Write the momentum equation.
  p = m × v

Step 3: Substitute values and calculate.
  p = 1200 × 15
  p = 18 000 kg m/s

Step 4: State the answer with direction.
  p = +18 000 kg m/s (eastward)

Key Points:
• Always include the direction of momentum in your answer.
• Use your sign convention consistently — state it clearly.

================================================================================
               SECTION 6.2: CONSERVATION OF MOMENTUM
================================================================================

HOOK — THINK ABOUT THIS:
In a game of snooker, when the cue ball hits a stationary ball, the cue ball
slows down and the stationary ball speeds off. Where does the motion "go"?
It does not disappear — momentum is transferred from one ball to the other.
The total amount of momentum in the system stays exactly the same.

RECALL:
• Momentum: p = mv (Section 6.1)
• In a closed system, there are no external forces acting.
• Newton's third law: when A exerts a force on B, B exerts an equal and
  opposite force on A (Module 4).

6.2.1 THE PRINCIPLE OF CONSERVATION OF MOMENTUM
-------------------------------------------------

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  In a closed system, the total momentum before an event is equal to the    │
│  total momentum after the event.                                            │
│                                                                             │
│       Total momentum before = Total momentum after                          │
│                                                                             │
│       m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂                           ... (6.2)       │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Where:
  m₁, m₂ = masses of objects 1 and 2 (kg)
  u₁, u₂ = initial velocities before collision (m/s)
  v₁, v₂ = final velocities after collision (m/s)

A CLOSED SYSTEM means:
  • No external forces act on the system
  • No energy is transferred in or out

IMPORTANT:
  • Momentum is conserved in ALL collisions (elastic and inelastic).
  • Two objects moving TOWARD each other have momenta of opposite signs.
  • If both objects end up moving together (perfectly inelastic collision),
    treat them as a single combined mass after the collision.

6.2.2 TYPES OF COLLISION
-------------------------

┌──────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Collision Type       │ Description                                        │
├──────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Elastic              │ Objects bounce apart; kinetic energy conserved     │
│                      │ Momentum conserved ✓   Kinetic energy conserved ✓ │
├──────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Inelastic            │ Objects deform; kinetic energy is NOT conserved    │
│                      │ Momentum conserved ✓   Kinetic energy NOT conserved│
├──────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Perfectly inelastic  │ Objects stick together after collision; maximum KE │
│ (coalescence)        │ lost as heat/sound                                 │
│                      │ Momentum conserved ✓   They move as one mass      │
└──────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

NOTE: Momentum is ALWAYS conserved. Kinetic energy is only conserved in
perfectly elastic collisions.

WORKED EXAMPLE 6.2
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Problem:
A car (mass 990 kg) travelling at 10 m/s collides with a stationary van
(mass 4200 kg). After the collision, the car continues forward at 2 m/s.
Calculate the velocity of the van after the collision.

Solution:

Step 1: Draw a before/after diagram. Identify known quantities.
  Taking right as positive.
  m_car = 990 kg,   u_car = +10 m/s,   v_car = +2 m/s
  m_van = 4200 kg,  u_van = 0 m/s,     v_van = ?

Step 2: State the principle of conservation of momentum.
  Total momentum before = Total momentum after

Step 3: Calculate total momentum before the collision.
  p_car (before) = 990 × 10 = 9900 kg m/s
  p_van (before) = 4200 × 0 = 0 kg m/s
  Total p (before) = 9900 + 0 = 9900 kg m/s

Step 4: Write the expression for total momentum after.
  p_car (after) = 990 × 2 = 1980 kg m/s
  p_van (after) = 4200 × v_van
  Total p (after) = 1980 + 4200 × v_van

Step 5: Apply conservation of momentum and solve.
  9900 = 1980 + 4200 × v_van
  4200 × v_van = 9900 − 1980 = 7920
  v_van = 7920 ÷ 4200
  v_van = 1.9 m/s (to the right)

Answer: The van moves to the right at 1.9 m/s after the collision.

Key Points:
• Always draw a before/after diagram — label each mass, direction and velocity.
• A stationary object has u = 0 and therefore p = 0.
• State the direction in your final answer.

WORKED EXAMPLE 6.3 — Objects Sticking Together (Perfectly Inelastic)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Problem:
A 2 kg trolley moving at 5 m/s to the right collides with a 3 kg trolley
that is stationary. The two trolleys stick together after the collision.
Find the speed of the joined trolleys.

Solution:

Step 1: Known quantities.
  m₁ = 2 kg, u₁ = +5 m/s
  m₂ = 3 kg, u₂ = 0 m/s
  After collision: they move together, combined mass = 5 kg, final velocity = v

Step 2: Conservation of momentum.
  (m₁ × u₁) + (m₂ × u₂) = (m₁ + m₂) × v

Step 3: Substitute.
  (2 × 5) + (3 × 0) = 5 × v
  10 + 0 = 5v
  v = 10 ÷ 5 = 2 m/s

Answer: The joined trolleys move to the right at 2 m/s.

================================================================================
                         SECTION 6.3: IMPULSE
================================================================================

HOOK — THINK ABOUT THIS:
Why do airbags save lives? Why do gymnasts bend their knees on landing?
Why does hail hurt more than rain even when the droplets weigh almost the
same? The answer is IMPULSE — and how force and time interact to change momentum.

RECALL:
• Momentum p = mv; change in momentum Δp = mv − mu (Section 6.1)
• Velocity is a vector; direction matters (Module 2)
• A resultant force causes acceleration (Module 4)

6.3.1 DEFINING IMPULSE
-----------------------

When a force acts on an object for a short time and changes its momentum, we
call this an IMPULSE.

IMPULSE is defined as:

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                                                             │
│                   impulse = force × time                                   │
│                                                                             │
│                   impulse = F × Δt                          ... (6.3)      │
│                                                                             │
│               Unit: newton second (N s)  or  kg m/s                        │
│                                                                             │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Where:
  F  = resultant force (N)
  Δt = time for which the force acts (s)

6.3.2 IMPULSE EQUALS CHANGE IN MOMENTUM
-----------------------------------------

The impulse of a force equals the change in momentum of the object:

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                                                             │
│           impulse = F × Δt = Δp = mv − mu                  ... (6.4)      │
│                                                                             │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Where:
  Δp = change in momentum (kg m/s)
  m  = mass (kg)
  v  = final velocity (m/s)
  u  = initial velocity (m/s)

WHY ARE THE UNITS THE SAME?
  N s = kg m/s² × s = kg m/s ✓  (same as momentum — the units are equivalent)

6.3.3 REAL-WORLD APPLICATIONS OF IMPULSE
-----------------------------------------

The key relationship: F × Δt = Δp

For a FIXED change in momentum (Δp is constant):
• LONGER contact time (larger Δt) → SMALLER force needed
• SHORTER contact time (smaller Δt) → LARGER force needed

This explains:

┌──────────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Scenario                         │ Explanation (using F × Δt = Δp)          │
├──────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Airbags in cars                  │ Increase Δt → reduce F on passenger       │
│                                  │ Same change in momentum, less force = safer│
├──────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Gymnast bending knees on landing │ Increases Δt → reduces impact force       │
├──────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Catching a cricket ball          │ "Giving" with the hands increases Δt →    │
│                                  │ reduces force on hands (less painful)      │
├──────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Hail vs. rain on an umbrella     │ Hailstones bounce back (Δp is larger)     │
│                                  │ → greater impulse → greater force felt     │
│                                  │ Rain splatters off (smaller Δp)           │
└──────────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────────┘

A tennis ball (mass 58 g) moves to the LEFT at 30 m/s and is struck by a
racket. The ball returns to the RIGHT at 20 m/s.
(i)  Calculate the impulse of the force exerted by the racket on the ball.
(ii) State the direction of the impulse.

Solution:

Step 1: Convert mass to kg and set sign convention.
  m = 58 g = 0.058 kg
  Taking left as positive:
  Initial velocity, u = +30 m/s (left)
  Final velocity,   v = −20 m/s (right, now negative)

Step 2: Write the impulse equation.
  impulse = Δp = mv − mu

Step 3: Substitute values.
  impulse = m(v − u)
  impulse = 0.058 × (−20 − 30)
  impulse = 0.058 × (−50)
  impulse = −2.9 N s

Step 4: State direction.
  The negative sign means the impulse is in the negative direction.
  Since positive = left, negative = RIGHT.
  The impulse acts to the RIGHT.

Answer:
  (i) Magnitude of impulse = 2.9 N s
  (ii) Direction: to the right (the direction the racket pushes the ball)

Key Points:
• If the object CHANGES DIRECTION, the sign of the velocity changes.
• Impulse acts in the direction of the applied force.
• The magnitude of impulse = magnitude of change in momentum.

              SECTION 6.4: FORCE AND MOMENTUM (NEWTON'S SECOND LAW)

HOOK — THINK ABOUT THIS:
You already know F = ma. But what if the mass changes, or you want to describe
force in terms of what it DOES to momentum? Newton actually wrote his second law
in terms of momentum — the F = ma form is a special case. Let's explore the
more powerful, general version.

RECALL:
• Newton's second law: F = ma (Module 4)
• Acceleration: a = Δv / Δt (Module 2)
• Change in momentum: Δp = mv − mu (Section 6.3)

6.4.1 NEWTON'S SECOND LAW AS RATE OF CHANGE OF MOMENTUM
---------------------------------------------------------

Newton's second law in its fundamental form states:

FORCE = RATE OF CHANGE OF MOMENTUM

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                                                             │
│                         F = Δp / Δt                         ... (6.5)      │
│                                                                             │
│               "Force equals the change in momentum per unit time"          │
│                                                                             │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

DERIVATION — Connecting F = ma and F = Δp/Δt:

  F = ma                           (Newton's second law)
  a = Δv / Δt                      (definition of acceleration)
  Therefore:
  F = m × (Δv / Δt)
  F = (m × Δv) / Δt
  F = Δ(mv) / Δt
  F = Δp / Δt    ✓

This shows that F = ma is a SPECIAL CASE of F = Δp / Δt when mass is constant.

6.4.2 APPLYING F = Δp / Δt
----------------------------

This equation is used when:
• The force and the time of its action are known → find Δp
• The change in momentum and time are known → find F
• The change in momentum and force are known → find Δt

REARRANGEMENTS:
  F = Δp / Δt     →   Δp = F × Δt     →     Δt = Δp / F

WORKED EXAMPLE 6.5
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Problem:
A tennis ball hits a racket twice, both with a change in momentum of 0.5 kg m/s.
During the first hit, the contact time is 2 s.
During the second hit, the contact time is 0.1 s.
Determine which hit experiences the greater force, and calculate each force.

Solution:

Step 1: Write the equation.
  F = Δp / Δt

Step 2: Calculate force during the first hit.
  F₁ = 0.5 / 2 = 0.25 N

Step 3: Calculate force during the second hit.
  F₂ = 0.5 / 0.1 = 5.0 N

Step 4: Compare.
  F₂ (5.0 N) >> F₁ (0.25 N)

Answer: The second hit experiences the greater force (5.0 N).
Even though the change in momentum is identical, the much shorter contact
time means the force must be 20 times greater.

Key Points:
• Shorter time → greater force (for the same change in momentum).
• This is why a sharp, fast cricket ball is harder to catch barehanded than
  a slow one — same momentum but shorter time means greater force on the hands.

Problem:
A 0.5 kg ball decelerates from 12 m/s to rest in 0.08 s when it hits a wall.
Calculate the average force that the wall exerts on the ball.

Solution:

Step 1: List known quantities. Set sign convention (right = positive).
  m = 0.5 kg
  u = +12 m/s (initial velocity)
  v = 0 m/s (final velocity — comes to rest)
  Δt = 0.08 s

Step 2: Calculate the change in momentum.
  Δp = mv − mu
  Δp = 0.5 × 0 − 0.5 × 12
  Δp = 0 − 6
  Δp = −6 kg m/s

Step 3: Calculate the force.
  F = Δp / Δt
  F = −6 / 0.08
  F = −75 N

Step 4: Interpret the sign.
  The negative sign means the force acts to the LEFT (opposing motion).
  The wall exerts a force of 75 N on the ball, directed to the left.

Answer: The wall exerts an average force of 75 N on the ball.

KEY CONCEPTS:

MOMENTUM (6.1):
• p = mv     [Unit: kg m/s]
• Vector quantity — direction matters
• Positive or negative depending on direction of velocity
• Stationary object: p = 0

CONSERVATION OF MOMENTUM (6.2):
• Total momentum before = Total momentum after (closed system)
• m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂
• Always conserved; kinetic energy not always conserved
• Objects sticking together: use combined mass (m₁ + m₂) × v

IMPULSE (6.3):
• impulse = F × Δt = Δp = mv − mu     [Unit: N s = kg m/s]
• For fixed Δp: longer time → smaller force (airbags, bending knees)
• Direction of impulse = direction of force applied

FORCE AND MOMENTUM (6.4):
• F = Δp / Δt   (Newton's second law — momentum form)
• Force = rate of change of momentum
• Shorter time → greater force for same Δp
• F = ma is a special case when mass is constant

KEY EQUATIONS:

Momentum:                   p = mv                              ... (6.1)

Conservation of momentum:   m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂        ... (6.2)

Impulse:                    impulse = F × Δt = mv − mu          ... (6.3)(6.4)

Newton's 2nd law:           F = Δp / Δt                         ... (6.5)

    Δp = −60 × 0.3 = −18 kg m/s
    Final momentum = 12 + (−18) = −6 kg m/s
    The ball now has 6 kg m/s of momentum in the OPPOSITE direction.
